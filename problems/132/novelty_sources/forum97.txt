FALSIFIABLE
Open, but could be disproved with a finite counterexample.
 - $100
Does every convex polygon have a vertex with no other $4$ vertices equidistant from it?
 #97 : [Er46b] [Er61] [Er75f,p.100] [Er87b,p.175] [Er90] [Er92e] [Er95] [Er97e] 
 geometry | 
 distances | 
 convex 
Erdős originally conjectured this (in [Er46b] ) with no $3$ vertices equidistant, but Danzer found a convex polygon on 9 points such that every vertex has three vertices equidistant from it (but this distance depends on the vertex). Danzer's construction is explained in [Er87b] . Fishburn and Reeds [FiRe92] have found a convex polygon on 20 points such that every vertex has three vertices equidistant from it (and this distance is the same for all vertices).
If this fails for $4$, perhaps there is some constant for which it holds? In [Er75f] Erdős claimed that Danzer proved that this false for every constant - in fact, for any $k$ there is a convex polygon such that every vertex has $k$ vertices equidistant from it. Since this claim was not repeated in later papers, presumably Erdős was mistaken here.
Erdős suggested this as an approach to solve [96] . Indeed, if this problem holds for $k+1$ vertices then, by induction, this implies an upper bound of $kn$ for [96] .
The answer is no if we omit the requirement that the polygon is convex (I thank Boris Alexeev and Dustin Mixon for pointing this out), since for any $d$ there are graphs with minimum degree $d$ which can be embedded in the plane such that each edge has length one (for example one can take the $d$-dimensional hypercube graph on $2^d$ vertices). One can then connect the vertices in a cyclic order so that there are no self-intersections and no three consecutive vertices on a line, thus forming a (non-convex) polygon.

 No counterexample with at most $10$ vertices.
Claim: there is no convex polygon with $n \le 10$ vertices (vertices in strictly convex position) in which every vertex has $4$ other vertices at a common distance from it. For $n = 7$ no convexity is needed: no $7$ distinct points in the plane have every point equidistant from $4$ others. With the counting bound $n \ge 7$ from TheAbandonedThinker's comment, any counterexample has at least $11$ vertices.
Method: let $Q_i$ be a set of $4$ vertices equidistant from $p_i$. Besides $|Q_i \cap Q_j| \le 2$, strict convexity gives two more constraints: no pair ${a,b}$ lies in three of the $Q_i$ (the three centres would be collinear), and if ${a,b} \subseteq Q_i \cap Q_j$ then $p_i p_j$ is the perpendicular bisector of $ab$, so $a$ and $b$ are separated by $i$ and $j$ in the cyclic order. Enumerating the incidence structures allowed by these constraints, up to dihedral relabelling, gives $0$ structures for $n = 7$, $1$ for $n = 8$, $16$ for $n = 9$ and $497{,}399$ for $n = 10$. Each structure is a system of $3n$ quadratic equations in the coordinates (with $p_0 = (0,0)$, $p_1 = (1,0)$), and each is shown to have no solution with distinct points. For $n \le 9$, and for the $54$ structures of the unconstrained $n = 7$ case, this is done by explicit Nullstellensatz certificates $\sum c_i f_i = 1$, re-verified by polynomial expansion; $5$ of the $16$ $n = 9$ structures have solutions, but only with coincident points ($I + (t \cdot G - 1) = (1)$ for a product $G$ of three squared pair distances, computed with sympy and with Singular). For $n = 10$ it is done by Gröbner bases: $487{,}240$ structures give the unit ideal and $10{,}159$ have solutions only with coincident points. sympy decided $32{,}979$ structures and Singular 4.3.1 decided $464{,}420$; $24{,}000$ structures were computed by both with identical verdicts, and sympy independently confirmed $9{,}503$ of the $9{,}649$ Singular exclusions and $4{,}998$ of $5{,}000$ sampled Singular unit verdicts with no disagreement (the remaining $148$ exceeded a time limit). The $n = 10$ part is therefore a Gröbner computation, not a hand-checkable certificate.
This is independent of the Lean-based $n = 9$ claim earlier in this thread.

 Thanks for publishing the certificates. Two things from working with them. I re-checked the expansion certificates independently: all 54 for $n=7$, the one for $n=8$ and the 11 for $n=9$. My code rebuilds the polynomials from each structure and expands $\sum_i c_i f_i$ in exact rational arithmetic, without a computer algebra system. Every identity gives $1$, and deliberately corrupted versions are rejected. The $n=8$ and $n=9$ structures satisfy C3 and are pairwise inequivalent under $D_n$. I did not re-check the completeness of the enumeration, the five $n=9$ classes decided by Gröbner bases, or $n=10$.
Your constraints also settle a case of the $k=3$ minimality question Erdős asked alongside this problem. Danzer's nonagon gives $n\le 9$, and my comment of 31 Aug showed $n\ge 7$. The proofs of C1 to C3 do not use $k$ (and C3 on its own implies C1 and C2). At $n=7$ they cut the 184,424 classes of witness-triple patterns left after my angle pruning and dihedral reduction to 2,340, and all 2,340 were refuted: 1,889 have no complex solution (Gröbner bases over $\mathbb{Q}$, in sympy and Singular) and 451 have no strictly convex real solution (z3 nlsat). So no strictly convex 7-gon has every vertex equidistant from 3 others, and for $k=3$ the smallest $n$ is 8 or 9. These are solver verdicts, not certificates.

 Three things: exact coordinates for Danzer's nonagon, a theorem ruling out one symmetry class, and a lower bound for the minimality question Erdős asked alongside this one. AlphaEvolve reached $k = 3$ but not $k = 4$ (Problem 6.53 of arXiv:2511.02864), so I worked the exact and symmetric side.
Coordinates. [Er87b, p.175] prints Danzer's nonagon as a figure with the relations $A_1A_2 = A_1A_3 = A_1B_3$, $B_1B_2 = B_1C_2 = B_1B_3$, $C_1C_2 = C_1A_3 = C_1C_3$, but no coordinates. Here is a convex $C_3$-symmetric nonagon satisfying exactly those: $(1,0)$, $(-1/2, \sqrt3/2)$, $(-1/2, -\sqrt3/2)$, $(-1/2+\sqrt3, \sqrt3/2)$, $(-\sqrt3/2-1/2,\ 3/2-\sqrt3/2)$, $(1-\sqrt3/2, -3/2)$, the other three of degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. All nine equidistant counts are exactly $3$, in exact arithmetic.
THEOREM. No $k = 4$ counterexample has dihedral symmetry $D_m$, $m \ge 2$, with every vertex on a mirror line.
A mirror meets a convex curve twice, so $n = m$ or $n = 2m$. The regular $m$-gon dies because on a circle two vertices are equidistant from $v$ exactly when they are symmetric about the diameter through $v$, giving multiplicity at most $2$. For $n = 2m$ the polygon is the alternating $2m$-gon with radii $1$ and $b$, convex exactly when $\cos(\pi/m) < b < 1/\cos(\pi/m)$; across that window the distances from a vertex strictly increase, so a distance is attained at most twice among mirror pairs plus once at the antipode.
Minimality. Erdős asked alongside this problem ([Er75f, p.100]) for "the smallest possible value of $n_k$". For $k = 3$ it is at least $7$: no strictly convex $4$-, $5$- or $6$-gon has every vertex with three others equidistant from it, so with Danzer $n_3$ lies in $\{7,8,9\}$. Two nearby results this is not: without convexity six points suffice (Erdős and Fishburn, Comput. Geom. 7 (1997), 207-218), by two similarly-oriented equilateral triangles translated by one side length, which is not in convex position; and TheAbandonedThinker's counting bound, run at $k = 3$, gives only $n \ge 4$. I did not settle $n = 7$.

 I believe I have a proof for this problem. The general case can be conditionally reformulated/reduced to the exact n=9 case.
The claim: The following three statements together reduce Problem 97 to the case \(|P|=9\).
1. Counting obstruction. Any counterexample \(P\) has \(|P| \ge 9\).
2. Surplus removable-vertex package. Suppose \(P\) is a counterexample with \(|P| \ge 10\). Fix a Moser triangle \(T \subseteq P\) and its three opposite caps \(C_1, C_2, C_3\). A closed cap-sum identity gives\[ |C_1| + |C_2| + |C_3| \;=\; |P| + 3 \;\ge\; 13, \]so some opposite cap has size \(\ge 5\). From any opposite cap of size \(\ge 5\) one can extract a vertex \(v \in P\) whose deletion preserves the counterexample property.
3. Strict-convex convention. Read "convex polygon" as "finite set of extreme vertices in strict convex position." (The alternative reading that allows redundant \(180^\circ\) boundary vertices needs a separate, routine degenerate-interface reduction.)
Theorem (conditional). Under (1), (2) and (3), Problem 97 is equivalent to the single finite statement: no \(9\)-point set in strict convex position is a counterexample.
So, the problem reduces to finding a 9-point counterexample in strict convex position, or proving the entire 9-point space does not contain a counterexample. I currently have axiom and sorry-free lean code proving this for n=9, modulo the actual computational results, which are taking a while to complete. Will share as soon as it's ready.

Current open frontier: The remaining unproven conjectures are both in the "descent" part of the problem (proving that all larger counterexamples can be reduced to size nine via removable vertexes):
1. Removable-Vertex Existence Theorem (conjectured).
Let A be a nonempty, strictly convex (convex-independent), finite point set in ℝ² with $A > 9$. Suppose A satisfies a property "PerVertexK4": every point of A has at least 4 other points of A at the same distance from it. Suppose further that no strictly smaller set with these same properties exists (minimality induction hypothesis). Then A contains a removable vertex: a point $x ∈ A$ such that deleting x preserves the PerVertexK4 property on the erased set.
2. Uniform surplus-cap removability (conjectured). Let P be a cardinality-minimal strict-convex PerVertexK4 counterexample. Choose a labeled triple of three distinct vertices of P (that I call a "Moser triangle") that is later promoted to the equilateral MEC-boundary packet, together with its three closed opposite caps C1, C2, C3. If some cap Ci has |Ci| > 4, then Ci contains a removable vertex.

 A small observation on the \(k=4\) case. Suppose every vertex \(v_i\) of a convex \(n\)-gon has some distance occurring at least four times among the other vertices. Choose four such vertices for each \(i\), and call the resulting set \(Q_i\). Let \(d(v)\) be the number of sets \(Q_i\) containing the vertex \(v\). Then \(\sum_v d(v)=4n\). For distinct centers, the corresponding circles are distinct and hence intersect in at most two points, so \(|Q_i\cap Q_j|\le 2\). Therefore \(\sum_{i<j}|Q_i\cap Q_j|=\sum_v \binom{d(v)}{2}\). By Cauchy--Schwarz, \(\sum_v d(v)^2\ge \frac{(4n)^2}{n}=16n\), and hence \(\sum_v \binom{d(v)}{2}=\frac12\left(\sum_v d(v)^2-\sum_v d(v)\right)\ge 6n\). On the other hand, \(\sum_{i<j}|Q_i\cap Q_j|\le 2\binom n2=n(n-1)\). Thus \(6n\le n(n-1)\), so any counterexample must satisfy \(n\ge 7\). In the extremal case \(n=7\), equality would force \(d(v)=4\) for every vertex and \(|Q_i\cap Q_j|=2\) for every pair of chosen circles. Thus the incidence matrix has the structure of a \(2-(7,4,2)\) design. I have not closed the geometry of this case; computational checks of the cyclic orderings suggest the remaining cases are highly constrained, but this is only evidence, not a proof.
